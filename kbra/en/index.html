Pagaya: what the rating reports say about credit quality

I went through 11 reports by the rating agency KBRA on Pagaya's securitizations1. The picture is mixed: 2024 loans are performing better than expected, but 2025 loans and the refinancing deals2 are losing money faster than forecast, and funding is also getting more expensive.

Five points worth highlighting


1

2024 loans are better; 2025 and the refinancings are running above expectation

Let's start with the good news. Loans issued from late 2023 through autumn 2024 are losing less than KBRA expected. In two deals from late 2023 it even lowered the loss expectation, which doesn't happen often. That fits what the company says about tightening underwriting after the difficult years.

The contrast with the past is sharp. In the 2021–2022 loans, losses reached 18%–24% of the pool, against an original expectation of 14%–16%. In the 2024 deals that are still active, losses stand at 11%–14% after about two years, and the distance to the red line is wide.

It's worth saying that the 2021–2022 deals are less relevant for a shareholder today. Pagaya has already recognized the losses on them, only 0.1%–8% of their pools remain, and they barely appear in the financial statements. They don't put the balance sheet at risk today and appear here mainly as a point of comparison.

Just one thing to keep in mind: most of these good deals are no longer being tracked. During 2026 Pagaya called them and refinanced the remaining loans into new deals, and there, as we'll see in a moment, the picture is different.

In the 2025 loans the picture is less comfortable. Six of the nine 2025 deals are losing faster than KBRA expected, and in two of them it has already raised the loss expectation. The magnitudes are still small, 5%–6% above the original expectation, but these deals are only a year old and more than half of the pool's life is still ahead.

The more interesting story is the refinancing deals, the ones marked with the letter R. Pagaya takes older deals, calls them, and packages the remaining loans into a new deal with a new loss expectation. Four of the five refinancing deals examined are running above expectation. In two of them KBRA raised the expectation by about a quarter in less than a year, and one, PAID 2025-R2, was placed on Watch Downgrade11 in September across three tranches.

That's surprising, because these loans came precisely from the "good" 2023–2024 deals. The likely explanation, in my view: those who remain in the pool after two years are the borrowers who couldn't prepay, so the tail is weaker than average. Refinancing also resets the clock: the original deal disappears from tracking, and it becomes harder to see how the whole vintage ended up.

2

Reported losses stop just below the red line

This is the point most worth knowing. Pagaya has the right to buy back13 delinquent loans from each deal, at par, up to 4% of the pool. A loan that was bought back doesn't count toward the deal's loss. In other words, the loss reported to noteholders can be lower than the true economic loss.

And what do the data show? Five consecutive 2025 deals passed the month-eight red line of 3.07% with reported losses of 2.79%–3.03%. In another deal the loss was 4.26% against a line of 4.31%, and in one refinancing deal it was exactly equal to the line. No 2025–2026 deal crossed the line in any of the reports.

I can't determine from the reports that Pagaya uses this right to stay below the line. It's also possible that the line is simply very tight in the early months. But the pattern is consistent, and KBRA itself writes that repurchases may prevent the protection mechanism from being triggered. And it's important to understand: the loss on such a loan doesn't disappear. It moves from the deal to Pagaya's balance sheet.

3

Funding is getting more expensive and the spread is eroding

In January noteholders accepted a weighted coupon of 6.15% on a new deal. By September they were demanding 7.54%. That's an increase of 1.39 percentage points in eight months, and it fits what we saw in the quarterly reports on funding costs and the pressure on FRLPC.

Pagaya responded by raising the minimum rate on the loans entering the deal, to above 20%. But note how this was achieved: the concentration limit on the riskier borrower group was widened from 15.3% to 21.1% of the pool since March. Higher rates come with riskier borrowers, and KBRA raised its loss expectation accordingly.

The net result: excess spread, the deal's first line of defense, fell from 12.73% to 11.46%, while the loss expectation barely moved. In other words, the new deals start out with less room for error than those from the beginning of the year.

What the data says
  • Coupon to investors by deal: 6.15%, 6.87%, 7.09%, 7.04%, 7.24%, 7.54% (2026-1 to 2026-6).
  • Excess spread: 12.73%, 10.42%, 10.66%, 10.96%, 11.70%, 11.46%.
  • Between 2026-2 and 2026-6: the minimum loan rate rose by 1.71 percentage points, and the loss expectation by 1.07.
  • Also in refinancings: the coupon to investors rose from 5.28% in 2026-R1 to 6.96% in 2026-R3.
  • In auto: excess spread fell from 10.73% at the start of the year to 7.44% in September, and rose to 8.94% in November.
  • The loan rate in the table is a minimum condition of the deal, not the actual yield.

4

The cushion at the bottom is thin

For each tranche KBRA calculates how much loss the pool can absorb before the tranche is hit. In the new deals, the lowest rated tranche is hit when losses reach about 20% of the pool, against an expectation of about 16.8%. The gap is only 3.35 percentage points. Put simply: it's enough for the forecast to miss by 20%.

Is a miss like that plausible? In the refinancing deals it has already happened: the expectation was revised by 24% and 25%. In the 2021–2022 loans the miss was 39%–71%, a level that would have hit the tranches above as well. In the regular 2025 deals the revisions so far are small, 5%–6%, but they are only at the beginning.

And below this tranche there is one more, the residual tranche14, which absorbs losses before everyone else. KBRA doesn't calculate a cushion for it at all. By my calculation, already in KBRA's base case about 83% of all the loss-absorbing capacity below the rated tranche is consumed. Whoever holds the residual tranche profits only if everything goes according to plan.

5

What this means for balance-sheet write-downs

In the third quarter Pagaya wrote down about $75 million of the value of its holdings. Only $43 million of that went through the income statement, and that's the credit loss. Its pace is steady, about $40 million a quarter for a year now. The rest was recorded in other comprehensive income and, in my view, stems mainly from the higher discount rate in the valuation, i.e. from the price of money (point 3).

The problem is timing. Today's write-downs come from 2024 and from 2023 and prior, the smallest vintages in the book. The 2025 vintage, the largest of all, took only $6 million, and according to KBRA the weakness is already there and in the refinancings.

My estimate: write-downs will continue at about $40 million a quarter, with upside risk. A large one-off event would require a miss on the scale of 2021–2022, and that would only happen in a sharp economic slowdown. An additional risk: the company's loss assumption stayed at 16.2%, even though KBRA raised its expectation on seven deals in July. If the company falls in line, it will show up in write-downs.

What I don't know: the vintage breakdown covers only risk retention, about $510 million out of about $1,100 million, of which $402 million is in the residual tranche. It is not known how much is held in each deal or how many delinquent loans were bought back. The November 10-Q should add detail.
What the data says
  • Holdings at the end of Q3: $1,101 million, against equity of $622 million according to KBRA.
  • Fair value assumptions: discount rate 15.6% (from 14.6%), loss 16.2% unchanged.

The bottom line

The KBRA reports don't tell a story of a credit crisis, nor a story of clean credit. The improvement in the 2024 loans is real. But in the 2025 loans and the refinancing deals losses are running above expectation, the rating agency is raising forecasts at an increasing pace, and funding is getting more expensive.

In my view the takeaway for a shareholder is that write-downs on holdings are a permanent part of the model, not temporary noise, and should be priced as such. The company is profitable even after them, and that's what matters. But the cushion at the bottom of the deals is thin, and if the US economy weakens, that's where it will be felt first.

What I'll be watching in the coming quarters:

  • Whether KBRA raises the loss expectation on more 2025 deals, beyond the two already revised.
  • What happens to PAID 2025-R2, on Watch Downgrade, and to 2025-R3, which is 0.83 percentage points from the red line.
  • The coupon to investors and the excess spread on the next deal, vs. 7.54% and 11.46%.
  • Quarterly write-downs in the company's reports, vs. about $43 million in the third quarter, and the volume of loans bought back from the deals.
  • The first data on 2026 deals coming out of their ramp-up period.

Glossary

The small numbers in the text point here
  1. Securitization · Pagaya packages thousands of loans into one bundle and sells it to investors as bonds. Each such bundle is a "deal", for example PAID 2025-3. PAID deals are personal loans, and RPM deals are auto loans.
  2. Refinancing deals (R) · Pagaya calls older deals and packages their remaining loans into a new deal, with a new loss expectation and trigger schedule. Marked with the letter R, for example PAID 2025-R2.
  3. Loss expectation · KBRA's estimate of the percentage of the loans in a deal that won't be repaid over its entire life (base-case cumulative net loss). When KBRA raises the expectation, it's saying the loans are worse than it first thought.
  4. Above / below expectation · For each deal KBRA sets a curve of how much loss is expected to accumulate by each month of its life. "Above expectation" means the loss accumulated to date is higher than the curve projected for this stage.
  5. Coupon to noteholders · The average annual interest the deal pays to those who bought its bonds. This is Pagaya's cost of funding.
  6. Excess spread · The gap between the interest borrowers pay and the interest the deal pays noteholders, after fees. This is the money that covers loans that aren't repaid before anyone loses principal, which makes it the first line of defense.
  7. Cushion (multiple) · How many times over KBRA's loss expectation the loss can reach before a given tranche starts losing money (breakeven multiple). A cushion of ×1.20 means it's enough for the forecast to miss by 20%.
  8. Tranches, and the most junior rated tranche · Each deal is divided into tranches. Senior tranches get paid first, and junior tranches absorb losses first. The most junior rated tranche (F-2) is the lowest one that received a credit rating.
  9. The red line, and the distance to it · A loss threshold set in advance for each month of the deal's life (a trigger). If losses cross it, all cash is redirected to the senior tranches, and the bottom tranches stop receiving cash flow. The distance to the red line is the gap between the actual loss and this threshold, in percentage points.
  10. Minimum rate on the loans · The lowest average rate the deal terms allow, under the worst mix the concentration limits permit. It's a floor, not the actual yield.
  11. Watch Downgrade · A notice from the rating agency that it is considering downgrading the tranche. It isn't a downgrade yet, but usually precedes one.
  12. Overcollateralization (O/C) · How much more the loans in the deal are worth than the total bonds issued against them. It's a cushion that absorbs losses before the tranches above it. When it erodes to zero, every additional loss hits the bonds directly.
  13. Repurchase of delinquent loans · Pagaya's right to buy from the deal loans whose borrowers are behind on payments, at par and up to 4% of the pool. A loan bought this way doesn't count toward the deal's loss, and the loss on it moves to Pagaya's balance sheet.
  14. Residual tranche · The very bottom tranche, unrated. It receives whatever is left after everyone else has been paid, so it is hit first. Pagaya holds some of the bottom tranches.
